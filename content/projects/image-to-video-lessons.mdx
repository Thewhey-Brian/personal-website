Most explanations of video generation start with a finished system: a huge
Transformer, a video VAE, a billion frames. It is hard to tell which part does
what. This course goes the other way. It starts with a 4×4 white square on a
32×32 black canvas, builds one mechanism per lesson, and ends with a
pretrained 2-billion-parameter generator that I adapt myself.

The through-line is one sentence:

> **Predict one future → represent many futures → learn a sampling path →
> let frames communicate → compress real video → train and evaluate a useful
> system.**

## How to use this post

Read the lessons in order; each one builds on the last. Every lesson has the
same five beats:

1. **The question** it answers.
2. **The setup**: data, model, and what is held fixed.
3. **Walk-through**: figures and looping clips, each with a *Watch for* note
   that tells you what to look at before you press play.
4. **The result**, with the numbers.
5. **Carry forward**: what to keep, and a question to check yourself.

Four rules hold throughout:

- **One factor at a time.** Objective, architecture, representation,
  conditioning and training scale are separate choices. Flow matching does not
  need a Transformer. A Transformer does not guarantee coherent motion. JEPA
  predicts representations, not pixels.
- **Controls and failures stay in.** Later lessons are not guaranteed to beat
  earlier ones, and several don't.
- **Honest splits.** In the toy lessons, starting positions are split before
  clips are generated (140 train / 40 validation / 45 test positions). In
  Lesson 5, whole trajectories are held out. In Lesson 6, clips are grouped by
  source video.
- **Diagnostics, not leaderboards.** Pixel MSE against one recorded future is
  a diagnostic, not a quality score: a different but plausible future can
  score badly, and a frozen frame can score deceptively well.

| Lesson | The question | What it shows |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 · Deterministic prediction | Why does a CNN average conflicting futures? | Prediction vs. the exact conditional mean |
| 2 · Diffusion | How can one image give different coherent futures? | Sampled futures, denoising, coverage |
| 3 · Flow matching | What changes with a velocity field? | Integration, solver steps, matched comparison |
| 4 · Temporal attention | How do future frames agree on a trajectory? | Ablation, scaling failure, JEPA |
| 5 · Real video from scratch | Can learned compression handle RGB video? | Reconstruction ceiling, robot futures, failures |
| 6 · Practical capstone | Can a small run improve a pretrained generator? | Paired base vs. adapter videos |

## Lesson 1 · Why a predicted video becomes an average

**The question.** When does one image determine a future, and when must a
predictor average alternatives?

### 1.1 Setup: a world small enough to understand

Every clip has a 4×4 square in eight 32×32 grayscale frames. The input is
frame 0, and the other seven frames are the targets. The image tells you
*where* the square starts. The dataset supplies the *motion rule*. In the
right-only dataset the rule is always one pixel right per frame, so the future
is determined even though one frame shows no velocity.

Starting positions are split **before** any clip is generated, so validation
and test positions never appear in training.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l01-dataset.mp4"
  width={560}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 1.1 · Generated training examples: input image on the left, the seven target frames playing beside it."
/>

> **Watch for.** The only thing the image carries is the start position. Nothing
> in a single frame says which way the square will move.

### 1.2 The model: one CNN, seven output channels

A four-layer CNN reads the image and outputs **seven channels, one per future
frame**. All channels share the same learned features and are trained together
against a whole target clip. There is no recurrence and no attention. The
network learns a fixed short-horizon mapping, trained with pixel MSE: compare
every predicted pixel with its target, backpropagate, update. Each run is 40
epochs and keeps the checkpoint with the lowest validation MSE.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-01_c08_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 1.2 · One input gives one output video. The training loss decides which video."
/>

### 1.3 When the future is determined, the CNN is fine

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l01-right.mp4"
  width={460}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 1.3 · A held-out start position: the recorded target and the CNN prediction move together."
/>

> **Watch for.** Both panels show a crisp square moving right. This is the
> baseline: when the answer is unique, MSE regression works.

### 1.4 Now make the future ambiguous

Keep each input image identical but pair it with **every allowed direction,
equally often**: two, four or eight. The model receives the image and no
direction label. It must return one video for all of those conflicting
targets.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-01_c13_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 1.4 · One held-out image with all its allowed trajectories: right only, left/right, four directions, eight directions."
/>

With MSE, the best deterministic answer is the **conditional mean**:

$$f^*(I_0)=\mathbb E[X_{1:7}\mid I_0].$$

For two non-overlapping futures, committing to one trajectory gets the other
wrong. Predicting half brightness at both locations splits the error and
lowers MSE. With four or eight alternatives, each square fades toward ¼ or ⅛
brightness, and overlaps add up. **An accurate average can be an invalid
video.**

> **Watch for.** (1) The CNN panel is nearly identical to the exact-mean panel,

### 1.6 Separate ambiguity from incomplete learning

Two different things can make the loss high, and they must not be confused:

$$\underbrace{\mathbb E\lVert X-f(I_0)\rVert^2}_{\text{prediction error}}=\underbrace{\mathbb E\lVert X-\mu(I_0)\rVert^2}_{\text{ambiguity}}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\lVert f(I_0)-\mu(I_0)\rVert^2}_{\text{distance from the mean}}$$

The generator is known, so the exact mean $\mu$ is an analytical benchmark. A
CNN that plateaus right at the benchmark has learned the ambiguous task
perfectly. It is the *task* that forbids a sharp answer.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-01_c16_0.png"
  width={720}
  caption="Figure 1.6 · Validation MSE per epoch for the four datasets, with identical training budgets. Each settles at its own floor: about 0 for right-only, roughly 0.007 for left/right, 0.010 for four directions and 0.012 for eight."
/>

### 1.7 The control: give the model the missing information

Concatenate two constant maps, `dx` and `dy`, to the image. Now the input says
*which* trajectory to predict, the conditional mean becomes that one trajectory,
and the optimal MSE is zero.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l01-control.mp4"
  width={460}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 1.7 · With direction supplied, the CNN predicts a sharp square again, for each of the eight directions."
/>

Making the network bigger would not have fixed Figure 1.5. The missing
ingredient was information, not capacity.

**Carry forward.** If you want *several* futures from one image, you need a
distribution and samples, not a better regressor.

**Check yourself.** Why can a faded two-square average have lower MSE than a
sharp, valid trajectory?

## Lesson 2 · From an average to sampled futures

**The question.** How can the same image produce several sharp, coherent
futures?

### 2.1 Setup: learn a distribution, then draw from it

Instead of one video $f(I_0)$, learn $p(X_{1:7}\mid I_0)$ and **draw** a video
from it. The data is exactly Lesson 1's: 140 / 40 / 45 positions, which gives
280 / 80 / 90 clips for balanced left/right motion. Future pixels are
normalized to $[-1,1]$.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l02-recap.mp4"
  width={640}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 2.1 · The starting problem: two valid futures, one deterministic average (exact mean and the direct CNN)."
/>

### 2.2 Add noise to a known future

Training starts from a real clip, moving left or right. Pick a noise level $s$
uniformly from 0 to 99, draw independent Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ for every
future pixel and frame, and mix:

$$x_s=a_s x_0+b_s\epsilon,\qquad a_s=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s},\quad b_s=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s}.$$

Here $\bar\alpha_s$ falls from about 1 (clean) to about 0 (pure noise) as $s$
goes from 0 to 99, so $a_s$ is how much signal remains and $b_s$ how much noise.
The clean first frame is kept as a condition. **One noise level applies to the
whole clip, and diffusion level is separate from video-frame time.** Left and
right use the same noise distribution, so noise carries no direction label.

### 2.3 Learn to denoise, then generate

The denoiser sees *noisy future + noise level + clean first frame* and predicts
the noise that was added. At generation time there is no target: start from
fresh Gaussian noise and apply 100 reverse steps to all seven frames together.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-02_c12_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 2.2 · Training starts from a known future and learns to predict the noise. Generation starts from pure noise and takes 100 reverse steps."
/>

There is no left/right decision head. **Why doesn't this average again?**
Because the denoiser sees the *noisy clip*, not just the image. A noisy state
that already leans toward the right-moving future is cleaned toward the right;
one that leans left is cleaned toward the left. The averaging that remains is
only among futures consistent with that state, not among all futures of the
image.

### 2.4 Make the sampler work: three controlled experiments

A valid objective does not guarantee a working sampler. Same data, 60 epochs,
100 reverse steps, left/right only:

| Experiment | What changes | Intuition |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Small 3D CNN, epsilon target | Starting model | Mix nearby frames and pixels |
| Video U-Net, epsilon target | Architecture | Broad spatial context, skip connections, explicit frame positions |
| Video U-Net, **v** target | Training target | Avoid dividing by a near-zero signal scale |

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-02_c16_0.png"
  width={720}
  caption="Figure 2.3 · The video U-Net: it downsamples space (32 → 16 → 8) but keeps all seven frames at every resolution; skip connections preserve the square."
/>

Why the target matters. For $x_s=ax_0+b\epsilon$, epsilon prediction
reconstructs $\hat x_0=(x_s-b\hat\epsilon)/a$, and when $a$ is tiny a small
noise error becomes a large video error. The alternative $v=a\epsilon-bx_0$
gives $\hat x_0=ax_s-b\hat v$ with no division. Diffusion's $v$ is **not** the
square's velocity and **not** flow matching's field.

Scoring uses a rule stated up front. The *nearest-trajectory MSE* of a clip is
its MSE to the closest exact clip among the valid directions. A clip is
*plausible* if that MSE is below 0.005, the closest direction is the same in
every frame, and the foreground is 12–20 pixels in every frame (the square has
16, with some tolerance). This is a toy heuristic, not a general video metric.
Evaluation is 8 held-out images × 8 draws = 64 clips.

| Model (left/right) | Plausible clips of 64 |
|:--|:--|
| Small 3D CNN, epsilon | 0 |
| Video U-Net, epsilon | 41 |
| Video U-Net, v target | **64** |

## Lesson 3 · Flow matching: learn a path to video

**The question.** What changes when we predict a velocity field instead of a
denoising target?

### 3.1 Setup: same task, same U-Net, new path

Keep the data, the conditioning and the U-Net layers. Change the path and the
sampler. The training budget is 60 epochs × 1,120 examples, batch 16, Adam at
0.001, with checkpoints chosen on fixed validation noise and times.

Three time symbols appear in this post, so here they are once:

| Symbol | Where | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $s$ | Lesson 2 | Diffusion step, 0–99. High means noisier |
| $\tau$ | Lessons 3–5 | Flow time, 0 to 1. **0 is noise, 1 is data** |
| $\sigma$ | Lesson 6 | Noise level, $\sigma=1-\tau$. 1 is noise |

None of these is *video-frame* time, which is always the seven (or 49) frames.

### 3.2 Construct a path from noise to a known future

Take a target video $x_0$ and independent Gaussian noise $z$, and define

$$h_\tau=(1-\tau)z+\tau x_0,\qquad 0\le\tau\le1.$$

At $\tau=0$ the state is noise, and at $\tau=1$ it is the target. The clean
first image is kept as the condition. **This is a constructed training path,
not a model prediction.** And $\tau$ is generation time, separate from the
seven video-frame times.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l03-training-path.mp4"
  width={420}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 3.1 · Constructed training paths: each clip is a fixed-τ state between noise and a known future."
/>

> **Watch for.** As τ increases, the noise fades and the square emerges. Nothing
> here is learned yet; it is just the interpolation we will train against.

### 3.3 Predict the velocity along that path

Differentiate the interpolation:

$$u_{\text{target}}=\frac{dh_\tau}{d\tau}=x_0-z.$$

This velocity is a **video-shaped tensor** describing how pixel values change
along generation time. It is not the square's $(dx,dy)$ and it is not
diffusion's $v$. Train $u_\theta(h_\tau,\tau,I_0)$ with velocity MSE. For each
example draw fresh noise and a uniform $\tau$. No ODE solve is needed in
training.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-03_c13_0.png"
  width={640}
  caption="Figure 3.2 · Validation velocity MSE over 60 epochs. A low loss checks optimization; generated motion has to be judged separately."
/>

The network learns the average target velocity *conditioned on the current
state, time and image*. That defines a field that transports a distribution of
states. It does not return one averaged finished video from the image alone.
**Why this avoids Lesson 1's problem:** the input now includes the noisy state
$h_\tau$. Different noise draws sit in different places, so the best velocity at
each state points toward one specific future. Averaging happens only among
futures consistent with that state.

### 3.4 Follow the field to generate

Start from fresh noise and integrate $dh/d\tau=u_\theta(h,\tau,I_0)$. This is an
ODE: a deterministic rule for how the state changes. An Euler step simply moves
the state a small straight step along the current velocity: $h\leftarrow h+\Delta\tau\,u_\theta(h,\tau,I_0)$. Each step updates all
seven frames together. No noise is injected after initialization, so with the
same model, noise and solver the path is deterministic. Different initial noise
can end in different motion modes.

As a sanity check on the equations and solver I first integrated an *oracle*
field that knows the target; it reproduced the target to about 10⁻⁶ at 1, 10
and 100 steps. That verifies the implementation, not learned quality.

### 3.5 Watch a direction develop during integration

The clean estimate at any time is

$$\hat x_0=h_\tau+(1-\tau)\,u_\theta(h_\tau,\tau,I_0).$$

For the known straight training path this identity is exact. For a learned
field it is a *diagnostic estimate*, not the result of integrating the rest of
the path.

### 3.6 Diffusion and flow side by side

Both start from the **same Gaussian tensor**. DDPM adds fresh noise between
updates, while the flow ODE follows its learned field. Equal update counts do
not mean equal noise strength.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l03-path-compare.mp4"

/>

> **Watch for.** Both start from identical noise. DDPM re-injects noise between
> updates, so its state stays grainy until late. The flow state is a
> deterministic path and sharpens steadily.

### 3.7 How many field evaluations are enough?

A straight *training* path does not imply one *learned* Euler step is enough,
since a single step cannot adapt to the changing state. On a separate
validation subset (8 images × 8 draws, identical initial noise):

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-03_c19_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 3.7 · Left: plausible fraction vs. network evaluations. Right: nearest-trajectory MSE vs. sampling time. One warmed timing measurement per setting on an RTX 5000 Ada."
/>

One step gives only 26/64 plausible clips, but **four steps already give
64/64**, and more steps shave pixel error slightly. This concerns left/right
motion on a small validation subset; it does not establish a general speed
advantage over diffusion.

Same image, same U-Net, same budget, consecutive draws from both samplers:

**A smooth generation path is not the same as smooth video motion.** There is

experiment is the simple velocity objective and a usable low-step sampler.

### Lesson 3 recap

| Method | What it learns | How it generates |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Direct CNN | A finished video from the image | One deterministic output; MSE averages ambiguity |
| Diffusion | Denoising quantities at noisy states | Iteratively samples a video distribution |
| Flow matching | Velocities along noise-to-video paths | Integrates a field from a random starting state |

### 3.10 Optional extension: beyond the trained horizon

Asking for 20 stretches its frame-position coordinate. It is *not* a trained
prediction of times 8–20. I compared two strategies on one held-out start whose
square stays on the canvas: generate 20 frames jointly, or generate seven, feed
the last frame back, and repeat.

The joint request gives slow, blurred motion (frames 8–20 MSE 0.0220) and
rolling tracks the target (0.00013). It also changes both the temporal

## Lesson 4 · How future frames communicate

**The question.** What does temporal attention contribute to a coherent video?

### 4.1 Setup: why leave the U-Net?

Lesson 3's U-Net gives valid left/right motion but biased frequencies, and only
1,090/1,440 eight-direction clips pass the toy checks, with per-position
success ranging from 53% to 94%. Those are limits of *this model and recipe*,
not proof that U-Nets cannot generate realistic video. Convolutions bring
shared local filters; attention brings adaptive long-range connections but
needs more optimization and data. Here is one concrete failure:

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l04-unet-failure.mp4"
  width={460}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 4.1 · An observed U-Net failure (draw 3): the square follows no single clean trajectory."
/>

### 4.2 Turn the future video into tokens

The token recipe:

- Split each 32×32 frame into **64 patches of 4×4 pixels**. Seven frames give
  **448 tokens** of 128 features each.
- Linear layers embed the noisy pixels and the matching clean-image patch.
- A small framewise convolutional stem adds local features. It never mixes
  future frames.
- Learned row, column and frame embeddings say where each token belongs, and
  generation time has its own embedding.
- The output layer predicts 16 pixel velocities per token.

Patchifying rearranges pixels; it does not compress anything.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-04_c08_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 4.2 · One frame becomes an 8×8 grid of patches; seven frames give 448 tokens, each carrying its patch, position and time conditioning."
/>

### 4.3 Alternate spatial and temporal attention

Attention computes $\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt d)V$: each token
becomes a weighted mix of others' features. **Spatial attention** lets a
frame's 64 patches talk to each other. **Temporal attention** lets the seven
tokens at one patch location talk across frames. Alternating them moves
information between places and times. I use three blocks with four heads. Both
directions are bidirectional, because the whole future is generated jointly.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-04_c10_0.png"
  width={720}
  caption="Figure 4.3 · Spatial attention connects patches within a frame; temporal attention connects frames at a fixed patch."
/>

The model has 763,088 parameters. The objective is Lesson 3's: the same linear
path and velocity target. **Only the architecture changes.**

### 4.4 Train on the same uncertain futures

Train from scratch for 60 epochs × 1,120 examples, then continue the selected
checkpoint for **120 more epochs** with the optimizer state restarted (180
total). The U-Net
baseline stays at 60 epochs, so improvement after continuation cannot be
credited to architecture alone.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-04_c15_0.png"
  width={720}
  caption="Figure 4.4 · Validation velocity MSE for the four direction sets over 180 epochs. Loss measures the field; samples test the motion."
/>

The Transformer did **not** win. A plain patch Transformer produced 0/64 valid
left/right clips at 60 epochs (that run is archived). With the convolutional
stem and longer training it improved, but at 60 epochs the U-Net still won in
every direction set, and on eight directions the Transformer reached only 1/64
valid clips.

### 4.9 Detour: JEPA, which predicts features rather than pixels

**The question:** can we predict the future in *feature* space instead of
pixel space, and does that remove the averaging problem? An online encoder
$E_\theta$ reads the observed frame(s), a predictor $P_\phi$ forecasts seven
future embeddings, and a teacher $E_{\bar\theta}$ encodes the real future
*only to build targets*. The teacher is an exponential moving average (EMA),
meaning a slow running average of the online encoder's weights, and "sg" means
stop-gradient: treat that term as a constant.

$$\hat Z=P_\phi\big(E_\theta(I_0),\ h\cdot E_\theta(I_1),\ h\big),\qquad L=\lVert\hat Z-\operatorname{sg}(E_{\bar\theta}(X_{1:7}))\rVert^2,\qquad \bar\theta\leftarrow0.99\,\bar\theta+0.01\,\theta$$

Here $h=0$ supplies one image and $h=1$ supplies two observed frames, mixed
randomly in training. A separate pixel probe decodes features so we can *see*
them.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l04-jepa-diagram.svg"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 4.11 · A context encoder predicts stop-gradient teacher features; the decoder is a separate visualization probe."
/>

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l04-jepa.mp4"
  width={640}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 4.12 · Eight consecutive cases: truth, feature reconstruction and predicted features."
/>

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l04-jepa-worst.mp4"
  width={460}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 4.13 · An explicit diagnostic selection: the two largest decoded forecast errors."
/>

| Context | Nearest-direction accuracy (how often the forecast is closest to the recorded direction) | Decoded pixel MSE |
|:--|:--|:--|
| One image | 12.5% (chance for 8 directions) | 0.0156 |
| Two frames | **99.7%** | 0.0031 |

The whole run took about 6.5 seconds. One image holds no direction evidence,
so a deterministic feature predictor cannot pick the recorded alternative. Two
frames expose the motion. The teacher probe's own reconstruction error (0.0002)
separates a poor forecast from a poor decoder. JEPA gives a predictive
representation for understanding and planning, but it still needs another
mechanism to *sample* sharp pixel futures. That job belongs to diffusion and
flow matching.

**Carry forward.** Attention shares information; it is not an automatic upgrade.
It matters when frames must agree, and it is data-hungry. JEPA answered its
question only in part: feature prediction works once the motion is observable,
but it does not sample pixel futures.

**Check yourself.** If every frame is individually perfect, why can the whole
clip still be wrong?

## Lesson 5 · Real video: compression and uncertain motion

**The question.** Can learned compression support generation of real RGB
futures?

### 5.1 Setup: the task, the data and an honest split

The [BAIR robot pushing set](https://www.tensorflow.org/datasets/catalog/bair_robot_pushing_small)
has real 64×64 RGB robot videos. Three official training shards give 768
trajectories, a fourth shard gives 256 for validation, and the separate
official test shard gives 256. No test trajectory enters training. Two 11-frame
windows per trajectory (starting at source frames 0 and 15) stay together.

The model predicts frames 4–10 in both settings:

- **One-image:** sees only frame 3.
- **History cue:** frames 0 and 3. This is a coarse *endpoint* cue, not the full
  history. It is supplied as $(z_3-z_0)/3$, the average per-frame change.

Actions and robot state are ignored.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l05-data.mp4"
  width={560}
  pixelated

/>

<Media

/>

### 5.2 Preserve the scene before generating motion

Train a frame autoencoder: **3×64×64 pixels → 8×16×16 latents → RGB pixels**.
It has 498,571 parameters. Each frame is encoded independently, with **no
temporal compression** and no pretrained perceptual network. Pixel MSE plus a
spatial-gradient loss trains it, and validation MSE picks the checkpoint.
Compression failure and future-generation failure are different problems, so
check reconstruction first.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l05-recon.mp4"
  width={460}
  pixelated
  caption="Figure 5.3 · Recorded clip (left) vs. its autoencoder reconstruction (right)."
/>

> **Watch for.** Compare the right panel with the left one for lost detail
> (arm edges, small objects). This is the *ceiling*: the generator can never be
> sharper than its decoder.

### 5.3 Model future changes in latent space

Freeze the autoencoder, standardize its channels, and model each future as a
**latent change from the last observed frame**:

$$r_j=\frac{z_j-z_3}{s_r}.$$

Train with flow matching on $r_\tau=(1-\tau)\epsilon+\tau r$ and target
velocity $r-\epsilon$. At sampling time, integrate and recover
$z_j=z_3+s_r r_j$ before decoding. The generator (1,329,288 parameters) is a
local 3D conv followed by four Transformer blocks that alternate spatial
attention over 256 positions with temporal attention over seven futures. The
observed latent frame is given at every future position. For the history
setting, the coarse cue is added; one-image mode gets a zero cue and an
explicit missing-history flag. Here $s_r$ is the training-set standard
deviation of latent changes, per channel.

## Lesson 6 · A practical capstone: adapt a pretrained generator

**The question.** Can a small training run improve a pretrained video generator
on a narrow motion task?

Here I stop training from scratch, and say so. LTX-Video 2B brings its general
image and video knowledge from pretraining. Any gain from my adaptation has to
be shown with held-out evidence.

### 6.1 Choose a visible, narrow behavior

The task is **hydraulic-press object deformation** from the public
[crush-smol pilot](https://huggingface.co/datasets/finetrainers/crush-smol).
The first image supplies the object and scene, the caption supplies the
requested action, and the model must keep identity while geometry changes.

- **Split:** 38 train / 3 validation / 6 test clips, grouped by original-video
  ID (7 / 2 / 2 source groups). Object independence is unverified, and captions
  are machine-written.
- **Retained frames:** 49 uniformly spaced frames covering the *complete*
  action, center-cropped to 512×320 and played at 12 fps (about four
  seconds). This normalizes action duration and changes original speed.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-06_c04_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 6.1 · Six actual training clips at retained frames 0, 24 and 48: approach, contact and deformation."
/>

### 6.2 Put the mechanisms together

A temporally compressed VAE turns 49 frames into **seven latent time slices**
on a 16×10 grid, so the Transformer processes 7×16×10 = **1,120 tokens**. The
first slice carries the image condition and the other slices are sampled
jointly. The VAE needs spatial sizes divisible by 32 and a frame count of
8k+1; 512×320×49 is a practical bucket, not a duration limit. This is a real
change from Lesson 5's independently encoded frames.

The text encoder, video VAE and base Transformer are **frozen and
pretrained**. Only attention-layer LoRA parameters train:

$$W_{\text{adapted}}=W_{\text{base}}+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,\qquad r=\alpha=16.$$

LoRA freezes the weight $W$ and learns a low-rank correction $BA$ (rank $r$,
scale $\alpha$). That is 14.68M of 1.94B Transformer parameters (**0.76%**).

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/l06-pipeline.svg"
  width={860}
  caption="Figure 6.2 · The pipeline, with frozen and trainable parts labeled."
/>

### 6.3 The exact training example

With clean latents $z$, noise $\epsilon$ and level $\sigma$, future tokens are

$$z_\sigma=(1-\sigma)z+\sigma\epsilon,\qquad v_{\text{target}}=\epsilon-z.$$

First-frame tokens stay clean, get timestep zero and are excluded from the
loss, so every example is conditioned on its first frame. This runs in the opposite time direction to
Lesson 3: with $\tau=1-\sigma$, $v=-u$, which is the same motion with a sign
flip. And this $v$ is linear-path flow velocity, *not* Lesson 2's
diffusion-$v$ (which comes from the $a,b$ mixing).

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-06_c09_0.png"
  width={820}
  caption="Figure 6.3 · One latent channel of a real training example at noise levels 0, 0.5 and 1. The observed slice (green border) stays clean."
/>

### 6.4 Measure feasibility, then train

A 20-update pilot used **11.4 GiB on an A40** at about 0.7 s per update. It
establishes memory feasibility, not quality. The main recipe is 1,500 updates,
batch 1, rank 16, and took about **10.5 minutes** of training.

<Media
  src="/projects/i2v-study/fig-06_c11_0.png"

/>

> **Read this.** The loss is flat and noisy. That is why quality is judged on

**Checkpoint selection.** I compared 500, 1,000 and 1,500 updates on the same
three validation images and two seeds, and fixed the choice **before any test
generation**:

| Candidate | Validation observation |
|:--|:--|
| 500 | Stronger action than base in several draws; the small-candy case often stops near contact |
| 1,000 | More action, but conspicuous press-geometry changes in case 00 |
| 1,500 | Clearest action on the hardest case; still invents debris and transfers appearance |

**I chose 1,500 updates**, and that adapter is "the adapter" in everything
below. The choice favors visible action and does not dominate every fidelity
criterion.

## What I took away

1. **Averaging is MSE's default under ambiguity.** If the task has several valid
   futures, the fix is a distribution and a sampler, not a bigger network.
2. **Diffusion and flow matching both represent alternatives.** Neither
   guarantees calibrated probabilities, uniform reliability or smooth motion.
   Count invalid clips instead of hiding them.
3. **Check controls before crediting the fancy part.** The U-Net beat the small
   Transformer, the ablation showed what attention is actually for, and the
   frozen frame beat the generator on MSE.
4. **Small illustrative batches mislead.** Several conclusions changed between a
   batch of eight and 1,440 draws.
5. **Separate what is pretrained from what you trained.** In the capstone the
   general visual knowledge is LTX's. Only the narrow, measurable change is
   mine, and its failures are as informative as its wins.

## Where this goes next

- A 75% / 25% left/right training mix: does the sampler reproduce the intended
  probabilities?
- A larger capstone audit: 12 source-disjoint cases × 4 seeds × 2 models,
  assessing identity, action, temporal consistency and severe failures
  separately, with a blinded preference check and bootstrap over cases.
- A temporally aware video autoencoder in place of Lesson 5's framewise one,
  with full multi-frame conditioning.
